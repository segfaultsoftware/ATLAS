# ATLAS SRD

ATLAS is a science-fiction tabletop roleplaying game about crews, ships, equipment, exploration, and risk. One player is the Game Master, or GM. The GM describes the world, presents challenges, and plays everyone who is not a player character. The other players each control one character and make choices for that character.

Most play happens through conversation. Players say what their characters do. The GM says what happens next. Dice are used when the outcome is uncertain and failure would matter.

## Core Ideas

- Characters are capable people, but their tools, ships, contacts, and supplies are central to what they can accomplish.
- Violence is dangerous. Combat should create lasting risk, resource loss, injury, death, or consequences.
- The game supports theatre-of-the-mind play. Maps can clarify position, but exact grid movement is not required.
- The GM can use tables and generators to create worlds, locations, jobs, hazards, factions, cargo, ship problems, and discoveries.
- ATLAS, the in-world interface, is also the player-facing app for character sheets, ship records, rules lookup, maps, logs, and automated table results.

## Characters

A character is defined by:

- Skills: what the character can do reliably.
- Gear: tools, weapons, suits, implants, software, vehicles, and other possessions.
- Conditions: harm, stress, debt, exposure, fatigue, legal trouble, or other problems.
- Relationships: allies, rivals, factions, patrons, obligations, and crew ties.

Skills are rated as a number. A skill rating tells you how many six-sided dice to roll when that skill is tested. A character rolls at least 1 skill die when attempting a check.

## Checks

Use a check when a character attempts something uncertain and the result matters.

To make a check:

1. The GM chooses the relevant skill.
2. The GM sets the target number of successes.
3. The player rolls skill dice equal to the skill rating, minimum 1.
4. Each skill die showing `3-6` counts as 1 success.
5. Each skill die showing `1-2` counts as 0 successes.
6. The player also rolls one Luck die, which is a `d10`.
7. If skill dice successes are equal to or greater than the target and the Luck die is `10`, the check is a critical success.
8. If skill dice successes are equal to or greater than the target and the Luck die is not `10`, the check succeeds.
9. If skill dice successes are lower than the target and the Luck die is `10`, the check succeeds.
10. If skill dice successes are lower than the target and the Luck die is not `10`, the check fails.

The Luck die can make a failed skill roll succeed, but only a check that would have succeeded from skill dice can become a critical success.

## Targets

Targets describe how many successes are needed.

| Target | Difficulty |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Simple under pressure |
| 2 | Standard challenge |
| 3 | Hard challenge |
| 4 | Severe challenge |
| 5+ | Extreme challenge |

The GM should set targets based on the task, opposition, time pressure, environment, and available equipment.

## Gear And Ships

Gear can change what is possible. A character may be unable to attempt a task without the right equipment.

Gear can affect checks by:

- Allowing the check.
- Reducing the target.
- Increasing the effect of success.
- Preventing or reducing consequences.
- Creating new options.

Ships use the same principle. A ship is not background decoration; it is a shared tool, home, liability, and resource. Ship systems can enable actions, reduce risk, suffer wear, or create problems.

## Outcomes

A failed check means the action ends in an unfavorable outcome. The character does not get what they wanted, loses an opportunity, suffers a cost, or faces a new problem. The GM chooses the result based on the situation.

A successful check means the action ends in a favorable outcome, but with a catch. The character gets what they wanted, but the result includes a cost, complication, reduced effect, delay, exposure, resource loss, or new risk.

A critical success means the action ends perfectly. The character gets what they wanted without a catch and may gain an extra benefit, such as:

- Faster completion.
- Better quality.
- Reduced cost.
- Extra information.
- Avoided harm.
- Improved position.
- A useful discovery.

## Contests

Use a contest when characters, factions, creatures, ships, or systems directly oppose each other.

The GM sets the target based on the opposing force. A stronger opponent usually means a higher target. If both sides roll, compare successes. The side with more successes wins. Ties preserve the current situation or create a cost for both sides.

## Harm And Risk

Consequences should follow from the fiction. Common consequences include:

- Injury.
- Lost gear.
- Damaged ship systems.
- Spent supplies.
- Heat from authorities.
- Debt.
- Exposure to hazards.
- Worsened faction standing.
- Time lost.

Combat uses the same check structure, but consequences are usually more severe. A character who chooses violence accepts the possibility of injury, death, escalation, retaliation, or permanent loss.

## Advancement

Characters improve through use, pressure, and consequences.

At the end of a session, mark skills that were used in uncertain or risky situations. A marked skill can become eligible for improvement when its use caused failure, cost, discovery, harm, obligation, or a meaningful change in the situation.

Gear, ships, contacts, and faction standing can also advance. Replacing, repairing, upgrading, stealing, buying, or losing important things is part of character growth.

## Rules Summary

```text
Roll skill d6s, minimum 1.
Each 3-6 = 1 success.
Each 1-2 = 0 successes.
Roll 1 Luck d10.
Critical success if skill successes >= target and Luck = 10.
Success if skill successes >= target and Luck is not 10.
Success if skill successes < target and Luck = 10.
Failure if skill successes < target and Luck is not 10.
```
